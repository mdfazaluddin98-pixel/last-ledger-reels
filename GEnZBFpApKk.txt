The War That Started Because of a Pig

In eighteen fifty-nine, the United States and Great Britain came dangerously close to fighting a war over a pig. Soldiers were deployed.

Artillery was brought in. British warships surrounded the island. And the entire crisis began when a pig wandered into an American farmer's garden. The island was San Juan Island, and both America and Britain claimed it. An eighteen forty-six treaty left the border ambiguous. By eighteen fifty-nine, American settlers and a British corporate farm were both there. Then an American farmer named Lyman Cutlar found a British owned pig eating his potatoes. So he shot it. Britain threatened to arrest Cutlar and remove the American settlers. The Americans responded by sending troops to protect them. On July twenty-seventh, Captain George Pickett landed with sixty-four soldiers. Britain answered with warships and Royal Marines. Within weeks, hundreds of American soldiers and dozens of British guns faced each other across the island. Then the situation became completely absurd. The Americans dug in with artillery. The British conducted military drills. Officers from both sides socialized together while their forces faced one another. But neither side fired a shot. British Admiral Lambert Baynes refused to turn the dispute into a war between two great nations over a squabble about a pig. Eventually, American General Winfield Scott negotiated a compromise. Britain and the United States would jointly occupy San Juan Island while the ownership dispute was settled. They stayed there for twelve years. Finally, in eighteen seventy-two, the German Kaiser arbitrated the dispute. He awarded the islands to the United States. The real conflict was over who owned the island. But one dead pig turned that dispute into an international military crisis. No American was killed. No British soldier was killed. No battle was fought. The only casualty was the pig.
